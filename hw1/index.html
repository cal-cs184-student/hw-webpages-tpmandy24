Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

To antialias triangle edges, we implemented supersampling by evaluating multiple subpixel samples per output pixel and averaging the results. We used the provided sample_buffer, which stores sample_rate color samples for each pixel, effectively acting as a higher-resolution temporary framebuffer. The size of the sample buffer is width × height × sample_rate, and it is dynamically resized when the framebuffer size or sample rate changes. During rasterization, instead of testing a single point at the center of each pixel, each pixel is subdivided into a uniform √sample_rate × √sample_rate grid. For each subpixel sample, we evaluate whether the sample point lies inside the triangle using edge functions. If the sample is inside the triangle, the corresponding entry in the sample buffer is filled with the triangle’s color; otherwise, it remains the background color. Once all primitives are rasterized, the resolve_to_framebuffer step averages the sample_rate subpixel colors associated with each pixel and writes the resulting color to the final framebuffer. This resolves the high-resolution supersampled image back to screen resolution.

Task 4: Barycentric coordinates

Barycentric coordinates are a way of expressing any point inside a triangle as a weighted combination of the triangle’s three vertices. Instead of describing a point using standard (x, y) coordinates, we describe it using three weights, commonly written in the form (alpha, beta, gamma). These weights represent how much influence each vertex has on that point, and they always add up to 1. If a point is closer to one vertex, that vertex’s weight will be larger. For example, if each vertex of a triangle is assigned a different color (red, green, and blue), barycentric coordinates let us smoothly blend those colors across the triangle. A point near the red vertex will look more red, while a point near the center, where the weights are similar, will appear as a balanced mix of red, green, and blue. In our implementation, we used barycentric coordinates to determine whether a pixel lies inside a triangle and to interpolate values across its surface. After computing the barycentric weights (alpha, beta, gamma) for each pixel, we used them to interpolate attributes such as color or texture coordinates. This produces smooth transitions across the triangle and enables accurate shading and texture mapping.

Task 5: "Pixel sampling" for texture mapping

Pixel sampling determines how we choose a color from a texture when mapping it onto a surface. Since texture coordinates ((u, v)) are continuous but texture images are made of discrete texels, we need a method to sample the correct color. In my implementation, I computed interpolated (u, v) coordinates using barycentric interpolation, converted them to texture space, and then sampled the texture using either nearest or bilinear sampling. Nearest sampling selects the closest texel to the computed coordinate and returns its color. It is simple and fast but can look blocky or pixelated. Bilinear sampling instead uses the four surrounding texels and interpolates between them to produce a smoother result. The biggest visual difference appears when textures are magnified or contain sharp details, nearest sampling shows noticeable aliasing, while bilinear sampling smooths transitions and reduces artifacts.

Task 6: "Level Sampling" with mipmaps for texture mapping

Level sampling is the process of choosing which mipmap level of a texture to sample from during texture mapping. Mipmaps are lower resolution versions of a texture that reduce aliasing when the texture appears small on the screen. Instead of always sampling the highest resolution image, the renderer selects a level based on how quickly the texture coordinates change across nearby pixels. In our implementation, we estimated the rate of change of the (u, v) coordinates to compute a level of detail value. We then selected either the closest mipmap level or interpolated between two levels, and applied the chosen pixel sampling method within that level. Pixel sampling affects how colors are chosen within a single texture level, with bilinear producing smoother results than nearest at a small performance cost. Level sampling improves texture antialiasing when textures are far away, but requires extra memory to store mipmaps. Increasing the number of samples per pixel provides the strongest overall antialiasing, but significantly increases computation time and memory usage.
