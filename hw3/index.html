Overview

In this homework, we built a basic path tracer step by step. We started with the geometry side by generating camera rays and testing intersections with triangles and spheres. Then we added BVH acceleration so the renderer could handle larger scenes efficiently instead of checking every object one by one. After that, we moved into lighting by implementing diffuse materials, direct illumination, global illumination with multiple bounces, Russian Roulette, and adaptive sampling. By the end, the renderer was able to produce much more realistic images with effects like soft shadows, indirect lighting, and color bleeding. One of the most interesting things we learned from this homework was how each part of the rendering pipeline depends on the earlier parts being correct. Small mistakes in ray generation, coordinate spaces, or normalization factors could completely change the final image, so debugging often meant thinking carefully about the math and the flow of light through the scene. It was also really interesting to see how much more realistic images became once indirect lighting was added. Even just a few extra light bounces made scenes look much softer and more natural compared to direct lighting only. Another important takeaway was how much performance matters in graphics. Without BVH acceleration, rendering even moderately detailed scenes was very slow, but adding a hierarchy made the renderer much more practical. Adaptive sampling showed a similar idea from a different angle, since it improved efficiency by spending more samples only where they were actually needed. Overall, this homework gave us a much better understanding of both the math behind physically based rendering and the practical techniques needed to make it efficient enough to use.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

In this part of the assignment, we implemented the core geometric steps of the rendering pipeline: generating camera rays, sampling rays through each pixel, and testing those rays for intersections with scene primitives. These components are essential because the path tracer first needs to determine how rays travel through the scene and where they make contact with geometry before any shading or lighting calculations can be performed. For ray generation, we implemented Camera::generate_ray(...) by mapping normalized image coordinates to a point on the virtual sensor in camera space. In this coordinate system, the camera is positioned at the origin and looks along the negative z-axis, while the image sensor lies on the plane z = -1. Using the horizontal and vertical field of view values, we converted the input coordinates from the unit image plane into the corresponding point on the sensor. We then constructed a ray from the camera origin through that point, normalized its direction, and transformed it into world space using the camera-to-world matrix. The ray origin was set to the camera position in world space, and its min_t and max_t values were initialized to the near and far clipping planes so that intersections outside the visible range could be ignored. For pixel sampling, we implemented PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(...) using Monte Carlo integration. For each pixel, we generated ns_aa samples using the grid sampler in order to obtain random sub-pixel offsets within the pixel area. These offsets were converted into normalized image coordinates and passed into camera->generate_ray(...) to produce camera rays. For each ray, we evaluated est_radiance_global_illumination(...) and accumulated the returned radiance values. After all samples had been taken, we averaged the accumulated radiance and stored the result in the sample buffer. This process approximates the integral of radiance over the pixel area and reduces aliasing artifacts by replacing a single deterministic sample with many sub-pixel samples. For triangle intersection, we implemented both Triangle::has_intersection(...) and Triangle::intersect(...). Our method solves for the ray parameter t together with the barycentric coordinates of the intersection point. Conceptually, this determines whether the ray intersects the plane containing the triangle and whether that intersection point lies inside the triangle itself. After solving for a candidate hit, we verified that the barycentric coordinates were valid and that t lay within the ray's allowed interval, defined by min_t and max_t. If the hit was valid, we updated ray.max_t so that any later intersections farther along the ray could be discarded. In Triangle::intersect(...), we also populated the Intersection structure with the hit time, the interpolated surface normal, the intersected primitive, and the corresponding BSDF. The surface normal was computed by interpolating the triangle's vertex normals with barycentric weights, which allows the rendered result to appear smoothly shaded instead of faceted. For sphere intersection, we implemented Sphere::has_intersection(...) and Sphere::intersect(...) by solving the quadratic equation obtained from substituting the ray equation into the implicit equation of a sphere. This yields up to two possible intersection values for t, corresponding to where the ray enters and exits the sphere. We selected the smallest valid t that lay within the ray's permitted interval. As with triangle intersections, we updated ray.max_t whenever a closer valid hit was found. For the surface normal, we used the analytic expression for a sphere normal, which is the normalized vector from the sphere's center to the intersection point. Taken together, these steps define the first stage of the renderer. A ray is generated from the camera through a sampled position on the image plane, tested against the scene's geometry, and, if it intersects an object, the corresponding geometric and material data are recorded. At this stage of the project, these intersections are visualized using normal shading, which serves as an effective debugging tool because it reveals whether camera rays are being generated correctly and whether the primitive intersection routines are computing the expected surface normals. To verify our implementation, we rendered several small .dae scenes using normal shading. These test images were useful for confirming both the correctness of ray generation and the accuracy of the intersection routines. Scenes with planar surfaces, such as the Cornell box, made it easy to inspect whether triangle normals were interpolated correctly, while scenes containing spheres provided a clear check that the analytic sphere normals and quadratic intersection logic were functioning as intended.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

In this part, we implemented BVH to make ray tracing much faster. Before this, every ray had to check every object in the scene, which made rendering slow once the scene got more detailed. BVH fixes this by grouping objects into boxes, so a ray can quickly skip over large parts of the scene if it does not hit the box around them. For BVH construction, we built the tree recursively. For each group of objects, we first found one bounding box that covered everything in that group. If the group was small enough, we made it a leaf node and stopped splitting. Otherwise, we divided the objects into two smaller groups and repeated the process for each side. The heuristic we used for splitting was based on the centroids of the objects' bounding boxes. We looked at the spread of the centroids along the x, y, and z axes, then chose the axis with the largest spread. We split at the midpoint of that axis, which usually gave a more balanced tree and separated the objects better. If all the objects ended up on one side of the split, we used a fallback split in the middle of the list so the recursion would not get stuck. For bounding box intersection, we used the slab method from lecture. We checked where the ray entered and exited the box along each axis, then combined those intervals to see whether the ray passed through the box at all. If the intervals did not overlap, then the ray missed the box and we could skip everything inside it. For BVH traversal, we recursively checked whether the ray hit the current node's bounding box. If it did not, we returned immediately and ignored that whole subtree. If it did, then we either checked the objects inside the leaf node or kept going down to the left and right children. For has_intersection(...), we could stop as soon as we found one hit. For intersect(...), we had to keep checking to make sure we found the closest hit. The biggest result from this part was the speedup. Without BVH, rendering larger scenes took a very long time because every ray checked everything. With BVH, most rays only needed to check a much smaller part of the scene. This made it possible to render large .dae files in seconds instead of waiting much longer. To test our implementation, we rendered a few larger scenes with normal shading. These were useful because they clearly showed the difference in performance once BVH was added. We also used the BVH visualization mode to check that the tree was splitting correctly and that the hierarchy looked reasonable. When we compared rendering times with and without BVH, the improvement was very noticeable. Without BVH, runtime increased a lot as scenes became more complex. With BVH, render times dropped a lot because the path tracer could rule out large sections of the scene early. Overall, BVH made the renderer much more practical for scenes with lots of geometry.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

In this part, we moved from normal shading to actual lighting calculations by implementing direct illumination. The main goal was to estimate how much light reaches a surface point and how much of that light is reflected back toward the camera. We worked with diffuse materials, zero-bounce emission, and two different methods for estimating direct lighting: uniform hemisphere sampling and importance sampling of lights. For the diffuse BSDF, we implemented DiffuseBSDF::f so that Lambertian surfaces reflect light equally in all directions. Since a diffuse surface has constant reflectance over the hemisphere, the BSDF returns reflectance divided by pi. This was a small part of the implementation, but it was important because it provided the material response used in all later lighting calculations. For zero-bounce illumination, we implemented zero_bounce_radiance to return the emission of the intersected surface. This means that if a camera ray directly hits a light source, the radiance returned is just the light emitted by that source. After updating est_radiance_global_illumination to include zero-bounce radiance, we were able to render scenes where the area light itself became visible. We then implemented direct lighting with uniform hemisphere sampling in estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere. In this method, we sampled random directions uniformly over the hemisphere above the surface. For each sampled direction, we traced a shadow ray from the hit point to see whether it reached an emitting surface. If it did, we used the rendering equation to add that light contribution to the outgoing radiance. This included the BSDF term, the incoming light, the cosine term, and normalization by both the number of samples and the sampling pdf. This method is conceptually simple because it directly follows the hemisphere integral, but it is inefficient since many sampled directions do not actually point toward a light source. The second method was direct lighting by importance sampling in estimate_direct_lighting_importance. Instead of sampling arbitrary directions in the hemisphere, we sampled directions directly from each light source. For each light, we used sample_L to get a direction toward the light, the distance to the light, the emitted radiance, and the pdf. We then cast a shadow ray to check whether the light was visible from the hit point. If the light was not blocked and was in front of the surface, we added its contribution using the same reflectance equation as before. This method is much more efficient because samples are concentrated in directions that are likely to matter, especially for small or point lights. For delta lights, we only needed one sample per light, while for area lights we used multiple samples and averaged them. To support both approaches, we updated one_bounce_radiance to call either hemisphere sampling or importance sampling depending on the setting of direct_hemisphere_sample. We also updated est_radiance_global_illumination so that it returned zero-bounce radiance plus one-bounce direct lighting. At this stage, the renderer could produce realistic direct illumination effects such as soft shadows from area lights and visible shading variation across diffuse surfaces. When comparing the two methods, the difference in noise was very noticeable. Uniform hemisphere sampling produced much noisier images because most sampled directions did not contribute useful light, especially in scenes where the light source covered only a small part of the hemisphere. Importance sampling gave much cleaner results with the same number of samples because it explicitly sampled the lights instead of wasting rays on irrelevant directions. This was especially clear in the soft shadows under the bunny and in the corners of the Cornell Box. As the number of light samples increased from 1 to 4, 16, and 64, the noise in the shadow regions decreased significantly, and the shadow boundaries became smoother and more stable. Overall, importance sampling was much more effective than uniform hemisphere sampling for direct lighting, both in image quality and in convergence speed. For our renders, we used scenes with area lights so that the difference in noise between the two methods was easy to see. In particular, comparing images rendered with 1, 4, 16, and 64 light rays showed that low sample counts produced visible grain in soft shadow regions, while higher sample counts greatly reduced this noise. These comparisons confirmed that light sampling converges much faster and produces cleaner direct illumination than uniform hemisphere sampling.

Part 4: Global Illumination

In this part, we extended the renderer from direct lighting only to full global illumination. The main idea was to allow light to bounce multiple times through the scene before reaching the camera. This makes the rendered images look much more realistic because surfaces can now be lit not only by the light source directly, but also by light reflected off other surfaces. This is what creates effects like softer overall lighting, light bleeding between colored surfaces, and more natural shadowed regions. For the first task, we implemented DiffuseBSDF::sample_f for a diffuse material. In Part 3, DiffuseBSDF::f only returned the BRDF value for a given incoming and outgoing direction. Here, sample_f also had to randomly choose an incoming direction wi and return the pdf for that sample. We used the cosine-weighted hemisphere sampler already provided in the diffuse BSDF class, which gave us an incoming direction in local object space. Since this was still a Lambertian material, the BRDF stayed reflectance divided by pi, but now sample_f also returned the sampled direction and its sampling probability. This was important because it allowed us to randomly continue a light path in a physically meaningful way instead of only checking direct illumination from lights. The main work for global illumination was in at_least_one_bounce_radiance. At a high level, our implementation first computed one-bounce radiance, which is the direct lighting already implemented in Part 3. Then, if the ray depth had not reached zero, we sampled a new incoming direction from the BSDF, transformed it from local space to world space, and traced a new ray from the current hit point in that direction. If this new ray intersected another surface, we recursively called at_least_one_bounce_radiance on that new intersection. The returned radiance was then multiplied by the BSDF term, the cosine term, and divided by the sample pdf. This follows the Monte Carlo estimator for the rendering equation and gives an estimate of the indirect lighting contribution. We also had to be careful about coordinate spaces, since the BSDF works in local object coordinates while ray intersections happen in world coordinates. We used the object-to-world and world-to-object matrices to move between the two correctly. We then updated est_radiance_global_illumination so that it returned zero-bounce radiance plus at_least_one_bounce_radiance. This meant that our final radiance estimate included both emitted light from light sources and reflected light from one or more bounces. We also made sure to initialize the depth of camera rays in raytrace_pixel using max_ray_depth, since recursive path tracing depends on the depth value being reduced correctly with each bounce. For Russian Roulette, we updated the recursion so that paths would terminate randomly instead of always stopping at a fixed depth. This makes the estimator unbiased, since long paths still have some chance of contributing, rather than being cut off completely. In our implementation, we always traced at least one indirect bounce when indirect lighting was turned on, and then applied Russian Roulette to later bounces. If the path continued, we divided the contribution by the continuation probability to keep the estimator unbiased. This helped control render time while still preserving the expected brightness of the image. The visual difference between direct illumination only and full global illumination was very noticeable. With only direct illumination, scenes looked flatter and shadowed regions were much darker because they only received light that came straight from the source. With indirect illumination added, light bounced around the room and filled in darker regions, making the image look softer and more realistic. In Cornell Box scenes like CBbunny, this was especially visible on the walls, floor, and the bunny itself. The scene no longer looked like it was lit only from one direction, and surfaces picked up subtle contributions from nearby objects and walls. When we rendered only the indirect illumination, the results looked dimmer but still revealed how much of the image comes from bounced light rather than direct light. This was helpful for understanding how path tracing builds up the final result. For CBbunny, the second bounce of light already added a noticeable amount of soft illumination, especially in areas that were not directly visible to the light source. The third bounce contributed even more subtle filling in of shadows and overall realism. These higher bounces are one of the main reasons path traced images look more natural than rasterized images, since rasterization usually does not account for this kind of light transport unless extra approximations are added. Comparing unaccumulated and accumulated bounces also made the role of each bounce clearer. In the unaccumulated case, each image isolated a single bounce level, so higher bounce images became dimmer and more subtle. In the accumulated case, each additional bounce built on top of the previous ones, so the scene gradually became more complete and realistic as max ray depth increased. By around 4 or 5 bounces, the image had most of the important indirect lighting information, and increasing depth further mainly made smaller refinements. For the Russian Roulette comparison, rendering CBbunny with max ray depth values 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 100 showed that the image improved quickly in the first few bounces and then changed more gradually afterward. The max depth of 100 did not look dramatically different from 4 or 5 in expectation, since most higher-order bounces contribute only a small amount of light. However, Russian Roulette allowed those paths to still be represented without requiring every path to continue to full depth, which made the method much more practical. Finally, comparing different sample-per-pixel rates showed the normal Monte Carlo behavior of path tracing. At low sample counts like 1, 2, or 4 samples per pixel, the global illumination result was very noisy because each pixel was averaging only a few random paths. As the sample count increased to 8, 16, 64, and eventually 1024, the noise decreased and the bounced lighting became much clearer and smoother. This was especially noticeable in soft shadow regions and on surfaces receiving indirect light, since those areas are harder to estimate accurately and benefit the most from higher sampling rates.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

In this part, we implemented adaptive sampling to reduce noise more efficiently during path tracing. Instead of forcing every pixel to use the same large number of samples, adaptive sampling lets the renderer stop early for pixels that have already converged and spend more time on pixels that are still noisy. This makes rendering more efficient because not every part of the image is equally difficult to estimate. Flat and evenly lit areas usually converge quickly, while edges, shadow boundaries, and regions with indirect lighting often need many more samples. The main idea behind adaptive sampling is to measure how stable a pixel's estimated radiance is as more samples are added. For each pixel, we traced samples one by one and kept track of the running sum of illuminance values and the running sum of squared illuminance values. Using these two values, we could compute the mean and variance of the samples without storing every sample individually. After every batch of samples, we estimated the confidence interval using I = 1.96 times sigma divided by square root of n. We then compared this value against maxTolerance times the mean. If the confidence interval was small enough relative to the mean, we treated the pixel as converged and stopped taking more samples for that pixel. We implemented this inside PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(). The function still begins by tracing rays through the current pixel, but instead of always taking exactly ns_aa samples, it now checks for convergence every samplesPerBatch samples. For each ray, we generated a sub-pixel sample, converted it into normalized image coordinates, generated a camera ray, and evaluated est_radiance_global_illumination. We added the returned radiance to the accumulated color for the pixel, and we also computed its illuminance using Vector3D::illum(). This illuminance value was used to update s1 and s2, which store the running sum and running sum of squares. Every time the number of samples reached a multiple of samplesPerBatch, we computed the mean, variance, standard deviation, and confidence interval. If the stopping condition was satisfied, the loop terminated early. At the end, we averaged the radiance using the actual number of samples taken and stored that value in the sample buffer. We also updated sampleCountBuffer with the true sample count so the sampling rate image could be generated correctly. The sampling rate image was especially useful for understanding how adaptive sampling behaved across the scene. Regions shown in blue used fewer samples because they converged quickly, usually in large flat surfaces or evenly lit areas. Regions shown in red used many more samples because they had higher variance, which often happened around geometric edges, detailed textures, or soft shadow boundaries. This matched what we would expect from Monte Carlo rendering, since those areas are more difficult to estimate accurately. When we compared the final rendered image with the sample rate visualization, the pattern made sense. Easy regions received fewer samples but still looked clean, while harder regions received more computation and therefore ended up much less noisy than they would have with a low fixed sample count. This shows the advantage of adaptive sampling: it saves work in simpler parts of the image and focuses effort where it matters most. Compared to using a uniform sample count everywhere, adaptive sampling produced a similarly clean image more efficiently by distributing samples unevenly across the scene based on convergence.
