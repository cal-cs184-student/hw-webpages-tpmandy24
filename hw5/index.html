Project Hail Minecraft: A Cinematic Shader Pack Inspired by Project Hail Mary

Team Members: Nicholas Angelici, Kathleen Hua, Mandy Pham, Sophia Prakasa

Links: Final Webpage | Presentation Slides | Demo Video

Abstract

For our final project, we created a custom Minecraft shader pack inspired by the visual world of Project Hail Mary. Our goal was to transform normal Minecraft gameplay into a cinematic science-fiction environment by using post-processing effects, color grading, haze, glow, dust, and localized light-based environmental changes. Rather than modifying the Minecraft engine, we worked through the Iris shader pipeline and focused primarily on the composite shader stage, where we could remap final screen colors and layer visual effects efficiently.

Technical Approach

Our shader work centered on the shaders/composite.fsh file, which reads the rendered Minecraft scene from colortex0 and outputs the final processed image. This allowed us to apply full-screen effects after the base scene had already been rendered. We implemented a red cinematic color grade by remapping RGB values, then layered additional effects such as glow, haze, vignette, glitter, dust, and atmospheric particles.

A key feature of our project was a localized environmental effect where the world gradually becomes redder as the player approaches a stream of light. This was designed to imitate the Petrova Line/Astrophage aesthetic from Project Hail Mary. Instead of rendering expensive true 3D particles, we approximated many of these effects in screen space, which helped preserve real-time performance.

Final Features

  • Custom crimson cinematic color grading
  • Post-processing glow and haze effects
  • Screen-space glitter, dust, and particle-like visuals
  • A large red swirled sky inspired by Project Hail Mary reference imagery
  • Localized red environmental shift based on player distance to a light stream
  • A modified End dimension inspired by Planet Adrian’s green atmosphere

Results

Our final shader successfully changes the mood and visual identity of Minecraft while maintaining smooth gameplay. The shader creates a dramatic red sci-fi atmosphere for the Petrova Line scene, with a bright central beam, red haze, glowing particles, and darkened edges that guide the viewer’s attention toward the cinematic focal point.

We also created a separate Planet Adrian-inspired environment by modifying the End dimension to have a green atmospheric look. This demonstrates that the same shader pipeline can be tuned to create different fictional environments by adjusting the color grading, haze, and sky effects.

Comparison

Compared to vanilla Minecraft, our shader produces a much more cinematic and stylized visual experience. Vanilla Minecraft uses a brighter and more neutral color palette, while our shader adds strong atmospheric mood through red and green color grading, bloom-like glow, and screen-space visual effects. The tradeoff is that our shader is less physically accurate than a full 3D particle or volumetric lighting system, but it is much more practical for real-time gameplay.

Performance

During testing, our shader did not introduce noticeable lag or major frame drops compared to regular Minecraft or standard shader packs. Because our effects are mostly screen-space post-processing effects, they are relatively lightweight. We prioritized real-time responsiveness over physically accurate simulation, which allowed us to maintain smooth gameplay while still achieving a strong cinematic look.

What We Learned

This project showed us how much visual style can be created through shader post-processing alone. We learned how Minecraft shader packs are structured, how Iris processes shader folders, and how the composite.fsh pass can be used to transform the final screen image. We also learned that visual effects such as haze, glow, dust, and color grading can communicate atmosphere and story even when they are approximations rather than physically accurate simulations.

One of our biggest takeaways was that small shader choices can create large emotional changes. A red shader can make the world feel dangerous and cinematic, while a green atmospheric grade can suggest an alien planet. This helped us understand how graphics techniques are not only technical tools, but also storytelling tools.
